I-PLAN Component Cordillera Administrative Region Regional Agriculture and Fisheries Investment Portfolio (2025 Edition)
Section I: Cordillera Administrative Region (CAR) at a Glance
Overview of the Region
The Cordillera Administrative Region (CAR) is a landlocked, mountainous region in Northern Luzon, bordered by the Ilocos Region and Cagayan Valley. It comprises six provinces and the city of Baguio, with terrain dominated by the Cordillera Mountain Range and rich watersheds.
Regional Political Map & Profile:
* 6 Provinces
* 2 Cities
* 75 Municipalities
* 1,178 Barangays
Topography & Environment:
* Topography: Elevations ranging from 1,000 to 2,500 meters above sea level. Generally steep and uneven terrain, with numerous valleys and ridges.
* Climate Type (PAGASA):
   * Type I - Abra, Benguet
   * Type III - Apayao, Mt. Province, Ifugao, Kalinga
* Ave. Annual Rainfall: 1,800 mm minimum (Abra) to 3,800 mm maximum in the rest of CAR
* Ave. Typhoon Occurrence: 20 tropical cyclones annually
* Soil Type: Mountain soils (53%), clay loam (17%), clay (9%), loam (4%), other soils (17%)
* Agro-Ecological Constraints: Soil Degradation, Climate vulnerability, Flood and soil erosion, Drought, Market access
* Sources: Planners Portal, RCES Environmental Report, PSA 2025, Land Resources Information Management System, FAO
SAFDZ Map Profile:
In the Cordillera Administrative Region (CAR), integrated crop-livestock-fishery zones are located in the low to mid-elevation areas of Abra, Apayao, Ifugao, Kalinga, and Mountain Province, where diversified farming systems are most viable. Agroforestry zones, found in the higher elevations across all six provinces, support sustainable land use and watershed conservation. The region’s major water sources—the Chico, Abra, Agno, and Amburayan Rivers—provide essential supply for irrigation, domestic use, and ecological services. Strategic livestock sub-development zones are concentrated in Kalinga, Mountain Province, and Apayao.

Section II: CAR's Prime Commodities
Overview of the Prime Commodities
The six provinces of the Cordillera Administrative Region (CAR) prioritized six commodity groups for development: high-value crops, rice, corn, cassava, livestock, and fisheries. From within these groups, eight commodities were selected for targeted investment—highland vegetables, coffee, heirloom rice, hogs, commercial rice, corn, cardava banana, and mango. These commodities were chosen for their strong potential to grow into robust industries, strengthen food sufficiency, and increase farmer incomes.
Commodity 1. Highland Vegetables
Industry Profile
Highland vegetables refer to crops cultivated in elevated areas, typically above 800 meters above sea level where cooler temperatures, distinct microclimates, and shorter growing seasons shape agricultural practices. These vegetables thrive in the mountainous region of the Cordillera. Common highland vegetables include cabbage, carrots, cauliflower, broccoli, snap beans, sweet peas, lettuce and bell pepper. Along with white potatoes, these crops are a major contributor to the Cordillera region’s agricultural economy, grown by smallholder farmers and marketed both locally and nationally. The major producers of highland vegetables are the provinces of Benguet, Mountain Province and Ifugao.

* Total area harvested: 25,825 ha (48% share to National area harvested; Rank 4th)

Competitiveness Vision: To leverage the unique semi-temperate climate and dedicated workforce of the Cordillera region for excellence in vegetable production. By adopting good agricultural practices, the region aims to enhance productivity through proper postharvest management and improved product quality, thus enabling the industry to meet market demand and ensure better incomes for all stakeholders in the vegetable industry.
Priority Constraints of the Highland Vegetables Industry
1. Poor Market System and Information: Without up-to-date market information, the vegetable farmers in CAR cannot predict the demand. Prices swing unpredictably, making it hard to earn steady profits. This also leads to reduced bargaining power of farmers and reduced farmer income.
2. High Cost of Inputs for Highland Vegetable Production: The production of highland vegetables remains costly due to expensive inputs such as seeds, synthetic fertilizers, machinery, fuel, and labor. As a result, many small-scale farmers struggle to maintain profitability, especially when market prices fluctuate or yields are affected by unpredictable climate conditions.
3. High Post-harvest Losses: The vegetable industry in CAR faces significant post-harvest losses as a result of poor harvesting techniques, inefficient transport systems, and substandard operations in packing facilities. These issues compromise product quality and reduce marketable yield, undermining farm profitability.

	Commodity 2. Coffee
Industry Profile
Coffee is a tropical evergreen shrub from the genus Coffea, cultivated for its beans, which are roasted and brewed into one of the world’s most beloved beverages. The Cordillera’s cool climate, high elevation (1,000–1,800 meters), and volcanic loam soils make it ideal for growing high-quality coffee. Farmers in the CAR cultivate Arabica, Robusta, Excelsa and Liberica coffee where these are most suited based on elevation and climate.
Regional Coffee Industry Situationer (2024):
* Total Vol. of GCB Production: 983 MT (3% share to National Production; Rank 5th)
* Total area harvested: 5,160 ha (4% share to National area harvested; Rank 4th)
* Bearing trees: 3.4M (4% share to National; Rank 4th)
* Demand Level: 1,447 MT
* Sufficiency Level: 68 %
* Production of GCB per province in 2024 (MT):
   * Abra: 17.17
   * Apayao: 8.80
   * Benguet: 118.24
   * Ifugao: 210.77
   * Kalinga: 590.08
   * Mountain Province: 38.37
Competitiveness Vision: An industry that is cost-competitive, aligned with global quality standards, resilient, sustainable, and environment-friendly; and provides continuous benefits to farmers, processors, traders, manufacturers, and exporters.
Priority Constraints of the Coffee Industry
1. Limited Accredited Nurseries: The limited number of accredited coffee nurseries in CAR is a major constraint (only 15 existing nurseries for Arabica and none for Robusta), limiting farmers’ access to high-quality, disease-resistant seedlings essential for boosting yields and improving bean quality. Without certified sources, expansion efforts stall.
2. High Cost of Inputs: Many smallholder growers struggle to afford quality inputs which leads to lower yields, poor bean quality, and increased vulnerability to pests and diseases. Without targeted support, coffee production remains economically unsustainable for many growers.
3. Low Productivity Because of Poor Cultural Management: As trees age, they produce fewer beans, become more susceptible to pests and diseases, and require more maintenance. High labor costs and an inadequate workforce reduce income. Coffee areas in remote areas have limited access to water, further affecting productivity.

	Commodity 3. Heirloom Rice
Industry Profile
Heirloom rice in the Cordillera Administrative Region is a diverse collection of traditional varieties cultivated for generations in the region’s rice terraces. Known for their unique flavors, colors, and resilience, these organically grown grains—like tinawon, balatinaw, and gilgilang—are deeply tied to indigenous farming practices and cultural rituals. Heirloom rice supports biodiversity, food security, and offers premium market potential, making it both an agricultural and cultural treasure.
Regional Heirloom Rice Industry Situationer (2024/2025):
* Total Vol. of Production: 30,000 MT
* Total area harvested: 13,178 ha
* Demand Level: 18,090 MT
* Sufficiency Level: 166%
* Share in National area planted to specialty rice: 28%
* Heirloom Rice production (MT) per province in 2025 (incl. upland rice of Abra and Apayao):
   * Abra: 4,637
   * Apayao: 13,985
   * Benguet: 507
   * Ifugao: 7,740
   * Kalinga: 320
   * Mountain Province: 12,155
Competitiveness Vision: To evolve into an industry where smallholders consistently produce enough rice to fulfill household needs and commercial/surplus production is substantially increased. Within the next five years, the industry hopes to develop a commercially driven production, milling, processing, and marketing capacity that can deliver sufficient volume for export marketing that is cost and quality-competitive without compromising local food security.
Priority Constraints of the Heirloom Rice Industry
1. Water Scarcity and Poor Irrigation Infrastructure: Reduces yields, compromises grain quality, and limits planting cycles. Traditional varieties depend on specific water regimes, and without reliable irrigation, farmers face higher costs and climate vulnerabilities.
2. Limited Sources of High Quality Seed: Without access to accredited seed centers or formal conservation systems, varietal purity and consistent yields are difficult to maintain. This limits organic certification and scaling up for premium markets.
3. Inadequate Postharvest Infrastructure: Lack of milling, storage, drying, and collection facilities hampers quality and marketability. Without centralized modern equipment, farmers struggle to meet quality standards for premium markets.

   * Estimated Cost: 1.5 - 5 Million

	Commodity 4. Hogs
Industry Profile
A hog (Sus domesticus) is typically defined as a domesticated pig weighing over 120 pounds. In CAR, hog production is primarily driven by backyard or smallholder systems raising native pigs and commercial breeds (Large White, Landrace, Duroc) suited to the terrain. Hogs make up over 75% of the region’s total livestock output, underscoring their importance to local food systems.
Regional Hogs Industry Situationer (2024):
* Inventory: 117,118 heads (Oct 2024) or 7,613 MT dressed meat produced in 2024
* Share to National Production: 1 % (Rank 14th)
* Demand Level for dressed meat: 25,760 MT
* Sufficiency Level for dressed meat: 30%
* Hog inventory (heads) per province as of October 2024:
   * Abra: 48,869
   * Apayao: 34,399
   * Benguet: 30,297
   * Ifugao: 27,133
   * Kalinga: 19,210
   * Mountain Province: 17,210
Competitiveness Vision: Following the African Swine Fever (ASF) outbreak in the country, the industry seeks to recover and rebuild by revitalizing hog production, replenishing swine stocks, and restoring market confidence in pork products.
Priority Constraints of the Hog Industry
1. Production Hampered by Disease Outbreaks: Outbreaks like ASF lead to high mortality, forced culling, and movement restrictions. Backyard growers are vulnerable due to limited access to veterinary services, biosecurity, and surveillance.
2. Inadequate Capital to Establish Hog Production Enterprises: Steep expenses for feed, veterinary supplies, housing, and biosecurity upgrades. Smallholders face limited credit access and minimal government support.
3. Insufficient Hog Processing Facilities: Lack of accredited processing plants limits value addition. Most hogs are sold liveweight or processed in small slaughterhouses that cannot meet food safety standards.
Priority Investments for Hogs
* Upgrading of Existing or Establishment of New Breeder-Multiplier Farms
   * Components: Breeder Module (10 gilts + 1 boar), Housing and facilities, Potable Water System (PWS), Disease diagnostic services, Biosecurity infrastructure, Quarantine checkpoint strengthening, Training, Admin Services.
   * Estimated Cost: 3.7 - 5.7M / farm
   * Coverage: Provincewide
   * Proposed Location: All provinces
   * Implementation Period: 2026-2028
* Establishment of Smallhold Feed Mill Facilities
   * Components: Basic Feed Mill Equipment, Civil works, Power & water system, Raw material storage, Packaging & weighing equipment, Working capital/raw materials, Labor, Training, Permits.
   * Estimated Cost: 5.3 - 9M
   * Coverage: Provincewide
   * Proposed Location: Ifugao / Mountain Province / Apayao / Kalinga / Benguet / Abra
   * Implementation Period: 2026-2028
* Establishment of Community-Scale Meat Processing Facility
   * Components: Building and civil works, Meat processing equipment, Cold storage and freezers, Water and power systems, NMIS certification, Training, Packaging & labeling equipment.
   * Estimated Cost: 3.6 - 25M
   * Coverage: Provincewide
   * Proposed Location: Selected municipalities in the 6 provinces
   * Implementation Period: 2026-2028
* Construction of AA Slaughterhouse
   * Components: Building & civil works, Equipment & machinery, Site development, Compliance & certification, Project management, Staff capacity building, Operational staffing cost/year.
   * Estimated Cost: 35 - 58M
   * Coverage: Municipalwide
   * Proposed Location: Ifugao / Mountain Province / Apayao / Kalinga / Benguet / Abra

	Commodity 5. Rice
Industry Profile
Commercial rice (Oryza sativa L.) is primarily grown in lowland and rainfed areas, especially in Kalinga and Apayao. It serves as a staple food and key source of household income. Commercial rice output has declined in recent years, highlighting the need for targeted support to improve productivity and market competitiveness.
Regional Rice Industry Situationer (2024):
* Total Vol. of Production: 299.4K MT (2% share to National Production; Rank 16th)
* Total area harvested: 93,509 ha (2% share to National area harvested; Rank 15th)
* Demand Level: 246 K MT
* Sufficiency Level: 122%
* Surplus: 53.4K MT
* Palay production per province in 2024 (MT):
   * Abra: 68,864.60

Priority Constraints of the Rice Industry
1. High Cost of Inputs and Limited Access to Finance: High burden from fertilizers, pesticides, seeds, labor, and water costs. Subsidy/loan programs suffer from insufficient coverage, untimely distribution, and low awareness.
2. Low Palay Prices and Unstable Market Conditions: Prices dictated by traders and influx of cheap rice imports depress farmgate prices, reducing profitability.
3. Inadequate Post-harvest Facilities: Reliance on traditional sun drying leads to crop loss, low grain quality, contamination, and reduced market value.

	Commodity 6. Corn
Industry Profile
Corn (Zea mays) is a vital crop in CAR. Yellow corn dominates with over 90% share (for feed/industrial use), while white corn is grown for human consumption. Ifugao has the largest cultivated area (34%), while Kalinga leads in total volume (average yields: 3.5 MT/ha yellow, 2.49 MT/ha white). Techno-demo farms show hybrid yields up to 9 MT/ha.

Competitiveness Vision: To transform CAR's corn industry into a competitive and profitable sector characterized by high-yielding, diversified farming systems, cost-effective production, minimal post-harvest losses, and enhanced value-added products, while preserving traditional white corn in the highlands.
Priority Constraints of the Corn Industry
1. Lack of Value Addition: Absence of processing facilities forces farmers to sell corn raw at low prices, missing opportunities for animal feed, grits, or corn flour.
2. High Post-harvest Losses & Low Mechanization: Inadequate drying/storage, manual harvesting, terrain constraints on mechanization, and unpredictable weather cause grain damage and spoilage.
3. High Input Cost and Low Yield in Major Areas: Production on fragile hilly slopes, extensive use of low-yielding F2 seeds, and extreme monsoon weather affect productivity.

Industry Profile
Cardava banana (Musa acuminata × Musa balbisiana, ABB group) is a cooking banana grown in mid-elevation zones of CAR in backyard or mixed farms. It holds value for food security, local dishes, and small-scale processing like banana chips.
Regional Banana Industry Situationer (2024/2025):
* Total Vol. of Production: 7,374 MT (0.30% share to National Production; Rank 16th)
* Total Area planted: 906 Ha (0.48% share to National Area; Rank 16th)
* Demand Level: 75,400 MT
* Sufficiency Level: 10%
* Volume of Cardava banana per province in 2025 (MT):
   * Abra: 2,038.01
   * Apayao: 1,869.50
   * Benguet: 631.10
   * Ifugao: 1,305.76
   * Kalinga: 1,112.76
   * Mountain Province: 417.32
Competitiveness Vision: To develop Cardava Banana into a resilient, value-added commodity that supports smallholder livelihoods through climate-adapted production, improved postharvest quality, and expanded processing (especially banana chips), while strengthening market access and branding.
Priority Constraints of the Cardava Banana Industry
1. Poor Accessibility to and from Production Areas: Steep terrain and lack of feeder roads/tramlines force manual hauling, causing high spoilage, fruit bruising, and reduced market value.
2. Limited Supply of Quality Planting Materials: Lack of disease-free, high-yielding suckers or tissue-cultured seedlings leads to poor genetic stock, uneven fruit size, low starch, and disease susceptibility.
3. Poor Post-harvest Practices and Facilities: Post-harvest losses reach up to 15% from rough handling and cuts. Absence of ripening chambers, ethylene scrubbers, and packing sheds leads to premature ripening, blackening, fungal infections, and shortened shelf life.
Priority Investments for Cardava Banana
* Establishment of a Tissue Culture Laboratory for Disease-Free Cardava Banana Plantlets
   * Components: Laboratory infrastructure, equipment & instruments, culture media, starter plant material, nursery & hardening facility, capacity building, QA, project management.
   * Estimated Cost: 7 - 8 Million
   * Coverage: Regionwide
   * Proposed Location: Apayao, Ifugao, Mt. Province
   * Implementation Period: 2026-2028
* Establishment of Cardava Banana Consolidation, Processing & Marketing Facility
   * Components: Upgrading existing facility to include processing center, equipment & tools, raw material procurement, packaging & labeling, branding, market linkage, training.
   * Coverage: Provincewide
   * Proposed Location: Apayao, Ifugao, Kalinga, Mt. Province
   * Implementation Period: 2026-2028
* Improvement of FMR and Alternative Transport Systems
   * Components & Estimated Costs: Farm-to-Market Roads (25M/km), Tramline (6M/installation), Footbridge (500k/meter), Footpath (1M/km).
   * Coverage: Province-wide
   * Proposed Location: Cardava banana production sites in Apayao & Kalinga

Commodity 8. Mango
Mango (Mangifera indica) is one of the major fruit crops of the Cordillera Region. The variety grown is the Carabao mango, prized for its sweetness and export potential. Mango thrives in lower-elevation areas of CAR, such as Abra and Kalinga where dry seasons align with flowering and fruiting cycles.

Priority Constraints of the Mango Industry
The mango industry in CAR faces several key constraints, including erratic climate conditions that disrupt flowering, aging and poorly managed trees, and widespread pest and disease issues like fruit flies and anthracnose. Farmers often lack access to quality planting materials and post-harvest facilities, leading to low yields and high spoilage. Market access is limited, with smallholders relying on middlemen and facing unstable prices.
1. Low Production of High Quality Mango for the Local and Export Market

   * Low mango yields in CAR especially for the export-favored Carabao variety (Mangifera indica) are caused by aging orchards, poor pruning practices, pest damage from cecid flies and anthracnose and inadequate irrigation facilities. These factors result in inconsistent fruit quality and reduced harvest volumes, making it difficult for farmers to meet both local demand and export-grade standards.
   2. High Post-harvest Losses

      * In CAR, many farmers still use traditional methods of harvesting and have limited access to ripening chambers, proper packaging materials, and market-linked logistics. These losses not only reduce income but also weaken the region’s competitiveness in fresh and processed mango markets.
      3. Limited Business and Market Development Skills among Farmers

         * Many mango farmers in CAR have limited business and market development skills, which restrict their ability to take advantage of the mango commodity as a source of additional income, analyze market trends, and negotiate fair prices. This gap hinders their capacity to scale operations, access higher-value markets, and diversify income through processing or direct sales.

	SECTION III: Multi-Commodity Interventions
Multi-Commodity Interventions Across Regional Prime Commodities
These proposed multi-commodity interventions are designed to create an enabling environment for investments that cut across all eight prime commodities of the region. They focus on policies, capacity-building, market systems, and risk management solutions that unlock the full potential of previously identified packaged interventions. By strengthening value chains through knowledge, skills, and regulatory support, these initiatives aim to reduce investment risks, improve productivity, and expand market access, creating attractive opportunities for public, private, and development sector participation.
Policy Development
         * Institutionalize ASF-free zoning and compartmentalization in swine-producing provinces
         * Streamline GAP/GMP certification for highland vegetables, banana, mango, rice, corn and coffee;
         * Streamline Balance Fertilization Strategy (BFS) in support to soil fertility management;
         * Provide local tax incentives for agri-processing enterprises handling heirloom rice, coffee, banana, mango; and
         * Streamline registration process of local inventions to allow local agri-equipment suppliers to provide farm machinery that is suited to the terrain of smallhold farms of the Cordillera.
Regulatory Service
         * Introduce biosecurity compliance certification for swine farms linked to financing and program eligibility;
         * Expedite movement permits for biosecure-certified farms; and
         * Establish a Regional Animal Health and Biosecurity Council to coordinate livestock disease prevention and rapid response.
Research Facility / Study
         * Conduct adaptive R&D for climate-resilient production of highland vegetable, heirloom rice, coffee, mango, cardava banana rice and corn;
         * Develop postharvest technologies and value addition for vegetables, coffee quality improvement, and rice milling efficiency; and
         * Advance biosecurity-enhancing technologies for swine farms.
Enterprise Development
         * Expand cooperative-led enterprises for highland vegetables, coffee, heirloom rice, pork, cardava banana, mango, rice, corn;
         * Mentor FCAs in product diversification for highland vegetables, processed cardava banana, mango, corn feeds and rice-based goods; and
         * Develop premium market-ready brands across commodities.
Market Linkage
         * Organize buyer-seller matching for mango processors, highland vegetable, heirloom rice, coffee, corn, and cardava banana cooperatives;
         * Facilitate banana, mango, and coffee producers’ participation in global trade fairs;
         * Develop a regional digital marketplace for all prime commodities;
         * Launch a sustained regional campaign promoting coffee, mango and cardava banana in domestic and export markets; and
         * Conduct commodity-themed investment forums targeting agri-tourism and specialty markets.
Mapping
         * Create a regional digital database for farm locations, production volumes, and postharvest assets across all commodities; and
         * Integrate disease surveillance mapping for swine into the agri-data platform.
Certification & Accreditation
         * Deliver TESDA-accredited training for coffee barista skills, mango processing, and banana product development;
         * Certify skilled workers in biosecurity management for swine farms;
         * Facilitate GAP certification for vegetables, mango, rice, corn, and banana producers; and
         * Facilitate GAHP certification for swine raisers.
Capacity Building & Support Service
         * Train FCAs on cooperative governance, quality control, and market negotiation;
         * Develop technical skills in postharvest management for vegetables, coffee, cardava banana and mango; and
         * Conduct leadership and enterprise management training for multi-commodity cooperatives.
Environmental Conservation
         * Promote climate-smart agriculture for rice, corn, vegetable, coffee, and banana production clusters;
         * Integrate agroforestry in coffee, mango, and banana areas; and
         * Implement soil and water conservation practices in sloping lands.
